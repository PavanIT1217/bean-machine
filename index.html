Skip the animation: outcomes are sampled straight from the binomial distribution, so the curve appears instantly.

Why a bell curve?

At every row a ball bounces left or right. With n rows and probability p of going right, the bin it lands in counts its right-bounces, so it follows the binomial distribution B(n, p) with mean np and variance np(1−p).

Pascal's triangle

The number in each cell is how many different left/right routes reach it — each is the sum of the two above. The bottom row, divided by 2n, is exactly the chance of each bin on a fair board.

The central limit theorem

Add up many small independent random steps and the total tends towards a normal distribution, whatever the steps look like. Sir Francis Galton built the first bean machine in 1873 to show it.

  • With very few balls the pile is lumpy — that's sampling noise.
